What the interview looks like

The induction PDF says they will ask about your code, and that "code you cannot explain will count against you." So the goal is clear: be able to explain every line of your three files, and make a small change when they ask.

What they will look at

Functionality
Does it work without bugs? Every button, every roll, on a laptop and a phone.
Code quality
Is it readable and organised? Yours is three short files with plain names.
UI/UX
Is it clean and responsive? Show it on your phone as well.
Understanding
Can you explain and change your own code? This one matters most.
Effort
README, hosting, a good commit history, maybe one extra feature.

For the Dice Roll problem they also list: correct and unbiased random logic, the look of the dice and animation, clean separation of HTML, CSS and JS, responsive design, and being able to explain how your code works.

How it usually goes

  1. Introduce yourself and the project. About one minute. Write yours in the Checklist section.
  2. Demo. Open the live site. Roll 1 die, roll 2 dice, show the history, then make the window narrow or open it on your phone.
  3. Walk through the code. They may point at any line and ask "what does this do?"
  4. Questions. HTML, CSS, JavaScript, randomness, Git.
  5. A small live change. Something like "keep only 5 rolls in the history."
  6. About you. Why the club, what you want to learn.
  7. Your question for them. Keep one ready.

Every panel is different, so treat this as a likely order, not a fixed script.

Three rules for the room

  • Point at the line on the screen while you explain it.
  • If you are not sure, say "I'm not sure, but I think…" and reason out loud. Thinking out loud is much better than silence.
  • The rules allow AI help. Be honest about how you used it, and show you understand by explaining.

    Evaluation point: correct and unbiased random logic

    Is your die fair?

    All of the randomness in your project lives in one line, inside getRandom():

    

      
    Watch the line work
    
      
    
        
    Math.random()a decimal from 0 up to 1, but never exactly 1
    
        
    × 6now it is somewhere from 0 up to 5.999…
    
        
    Math.floor( … )cut off everything after the decimal point: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5
    
        
    + 1shift 0–5 up to 1–6, the faces of a real die
    
        
    
          
    
          
    face 1
    face 2
    face 3
    face 4
    face 5
    face 6
    
          
    0123456
    
        
    
        
    
          
          The orange line shows where Math.random() * 6 landed.
        
    
      
    
      
    The strip goes from 0 to 6, and every face owns a piece exactly 1 wide. Math.random() is spread evenly, so it is just as likely to land in any piece. Equal pieces mean equal chances: each face has a 1 in 6 chance. That is the whole reason your die is fair.
    

      
    Test it: roll thousands of times
    
      
    Here is your getRandom() next to a common mistake, both rolled the same number of times. The black line is a perfectly fair share.
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    On the left, every bar stays close to the line. They are never exactly equal, because the rolls are random, but the more you roll, the closer they get. On the right, faces 1 and 6 come up only about half as often. Math.round gives 1 only for numbers from 0 to 0.5 and 6 only for 4.5 to 5: pieces half as wide as the others.
    

      
    Is it truly random?
    
      
    Not quite. Math.random() is pseudo-random: the browser calculates it with a formula. The numbers look random and are spread evenly, which is perfect for a dice game. For passwords or anything secret you would use crypto.getRandomValues() instead.
